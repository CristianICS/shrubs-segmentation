\begin{abstract}
Accurate mapping of shrub vegetation is important for monitoring post-fire ecosystem recovery, but individual shrub delineation remains challenging because of heterogeneous canopy structure, limited reference data, and spectral similarity with other vegetation. This study evaluated the transferability of two deep-learning instance-segmentation approaches, Mask R-CNN and the promptable SAM 3 model, for mapping Mediterranean post-fire shrub vegetation from very-high-resolution UAV RGB imagery. Both models were adapted using the JuniperMapper dataset and evaluated on its photo-interpretation and field-work subsets before deployment in a fire-affected Mediterranean shrubland in Encinacorba, northeastern Spain. UAV LiDAR data were additionally used to derive a Canopy Height Model (CHM) and refine the RGB-based predictions by removing vegetation identified as trees.

Within the JuniperMapper domain, both models achieved high segmentation accuracy, although domain-adapted SAM 3 consistently outperformed Mask R-CNN. At their selected operating points, SAM 3 reached an F1-score of \qty{78.3}{\percent} on the field-work dataset, compared with \qty{75.2}{\percent} for Mask R-CNN. However, both models transferred poorly to the independent post-fire study area, where recall decreased to \qty{3.4}{\percent} for SAM 3 and \qty{1.7}{\percent} for Mask R-CNN. The models preferentially detected taller vegetation and failed to identify most low-stature shrubs, indicating a substantial ecological and structural domain shift between the training and deployment environments. LiDAR-based refinement effectively reduced tree commission, from \qty{61.9}{\percent} to \qty{14.3}{\percent} for SAM 3 and from \qty{28.6}{\percent} to \qty{4.8}{\percent} for Mask R-CNN, without removing matched shrub predictions. Nevertheless, overall F1 improved only marginally because LiDAR filtering could remove false positives but could not recover shrubs missed by the RGB models.

These results show that high performance within a source dataset does not guarantee successful transfer to structurally different shrub communities. Representative local training data appear to be more important than additional post-processing for mapping heterogeneous post-fire shrublands, while LiDAR provides useful complementary information for reducing tree-related commission errors.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Accurate characterization of forest cover is essential for sustainable ecosystem management, land-use planning, ecological research, and the design of restoration and risk-prevention measures \citep{chazdon2008beyond, townshend2012global}. In Mediterranean ecosystems, shrublands are also particularly important because they protect soils from erosion, support biodiversity, contribute to vegetation succession, and influence fuel accumulation and wildfire behavior \citep{keeley2005determinants}.

Mediterranean shrublands consist mainly of low, densely branched woody vegetation adapted to long, dry summers, mild winters, and recurrent fire. Many shrub species recover rapidly after fire through vegetative resprouting or fire-stimulated germination, making shrubland development an important indicator of post-fire regeneration \citep{keeley1986resilience,pausas2008wildfires}. In fire-affected areas, accurately mapping shrub vegetation is therefore necessary to assess ecological recovery, estimate biomass accumulation, evaluate future fire risk, and plan restoration and forest-management actions. However, shrubland identification remains challenging because of its high spatial and spectral heterogeneity and its spectral similarity to other land-cover types \citep{liu2026underestimated,west2025landsat}.

Remote sensing has substantially improved the detection and characterization of vegetation by enabling observations over large areas at high spatial, spectral, and temporal resolutions. Traditional land-cover mapping has relied on visual interpretation, spectral classification, and manual digitization, as in regional programs such as CORINE \citep{grekousis2015overview}. Object-Based Image Analysis has also been applied by classifying imagery into homogeneous regions and assigning classes according to spectral, textural, and spatial properties \citep{blaschke2010object,soubry2022mapping}. Although these approaches can produce reliable maps, their performance depends heavily on operator expertise, parameter selection, and analysis scale, and they may have difficulty representing structurally complex and heterogeneous vegetation \citep{mishra2014mapping}.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning (DL), have created new opportunities for overcoming these limitations. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) can automatically extract hierarchical and multiscale features from imagery and have improved the classification and segmentation of vegetation and land-cover categories in complex environments \citep{abdi2020land,kattenborn2021review}. Architectures such as U-Net, Mask R-CNN, and DeepLab have been successfully used to delineate forest cover, shrubland, and individual tree structures from high- and very-high-resolution imagery \citep{he2023improvements,guirado2017deeplearning}.

Despite these advances, the automatic delineation of heterogeneous shrub vegetation in post-fire environments remains insufficiently explored. Addressing this gap could improve the efficiency and reliability of post-fire vegetation monitoring while reducing the time requirements and subjectivity associated with manual interpretation. However, the application of CNNs relies on substantial amounts of labeled training data, which are difficult to obtain in highly diverse ecosystems, where plant delineation is itself more difficult \citep{zhang2020identifying}. Post-fire environments add a further challenge, because they are dominated by polymorphic shrubs in which crown overlap and splitting may occur \citep{dong2019progressive}.

To alleviate this limitation, transfer-learning strategies can increase the applicability of CNNs by reusing features learned from previously analyzed datasets \citep{tan2018survey}. This approach reduces both computational requirements and the amount of labeled training data required, which is particularly valuable in environmental applications where reference data are often limited.

Transfer learning addresses the scarcity of labels, but it does not change the information contained in the imagery. RGB images record only the spectral and textural properties of the canopy, so shrubs remain difficult to separate from spectrally similar land-cover types and adjacent crowns can be hard to distinguish. LiDAR provides structural information that is unavailable from RGB imagery, such as vegetation height and three-dimensional canopy form. Previous studies have shown that combining spectral, geometric, photogrammetric, and LiDAR-derived information can improve shrub and tree classification. For example, \citet{carbonellrivera2022classification} reported an overall accuracy of approximately \qty{96}{\percent} when classifying Mediterranean shrub and tree species using UAV-derived point clouds and supervised classifiers. Other studies have obtained promising results through multispectral classification, object-based analysis, and rule-based methods incorporating vegetation-height information \citep{uyeda2020vegetation,zahidi2015object}.

This study evaluates both ideas in a post-fire shrub-mapping application. Its aim is to assess the effectiveness of very-high-spatial-resolution RGB imagery for the automated mapping of shrub cover in a fire-affected forest area, using transfer learning to compensate for limited labeled data and LiDAR to add structural information. Following the transfer-learning approach, pretrained models are fine-tuned on the JuniperMapper dataset \citep{khaldi2023junipermapper} and their performance in Mediterranean shrub mapping is assessed. The study comprises one Mask R-CNN implementation and one promptable transformer-based segmentation model. The Detectron2 implementation of Mask R-CNN reproduced the configuration used in the original JuniperMapper study \citep{khaldi2024individual}, providing a literature-based reference for comparison. The SAM 3 promptable model \citep{carion2026sam3} is also investigated. The classifications generated by the RGB-based models are combined with UAV LiDAR-derived point-cloud data to determine whether the integration of structural information improves shrub-mapping accuracy.

\section{Methodology}

The workflow of this research is explained in Figure~\ref{fig:methods}. First, the data were collected in the field, i.e., the drone flight and the shrub data within the locations used to validate the models. Then the data were processed, and the shrub masks were digitized. The models were trained and internally evaluated with the JuniperMapper dataset. Finally, the best configuration of each model was deployed in the study area and the metrics were computed using ground reference data. After the creation of the shrub maps, the refinement operation was applied to try to improve the masks and the accuracy metrics were recomputed. \subsection{Study area}

The study area is located within the Sierra de Algairén (Figure~\ref{fig:location}), which reaches elevations of approximately \qty{1000}{\metre} above sea level and is characterized by a Mediterranean climate with continental influences. The climate is characterized by cold winters and hot, dry summers, with marked temperature fluctuations throughout the year. The mean annual temperature is approximately \qty{12}{\celsius}, with winter minima that can fall to \qty{-2}{\celsius} and summer maxima frequently exceeding \qty{30}{\celsius}. Mean annual precipitation ranges between \qty{500}{\milli\metre} and \qty{600}{\milli\metre} and is concentrated mainly in spring and autumn. This precipitation regime, together with the dryness of the summer months, promotes soil desiccation and increases the risk of forest fires.

The area selected for model validation is located in the municipality of Encinacorba, Zaragoza (Aragón), within the area affected by the wildfire that occurred on 29 July 2003. A total area of \qty{267.4}{\hectare} was burned. More specifically, the study area comprises \qty{13.3}{\hectare} with a south-facing aspect that was completely burned during the wildfire (Figure~\ref{fig:location}). Its steep mean slope gradient of \qty{21.5}{\degree} has hindered post-fire vegetation recovery.

At present, the study area is dominated by low- to medium-stature shrubland. The most abundant species include \textit{Thymus vulgaris} L., \textit{Sedum sediforme} Jacq., \textit{Lavandula stoechas} L., and \textit{Cistus laurifolius} L. Scattered woody vegetation is also present, including \textit{Quercus ilex} L., \textit{Pinus pinaster} Aiton, and \textit{Crataegus monogyna} Jacq. A high proportion of bare soil and necromass remains.

\subsection{Data acquisition}

The study area was flown with a DJI Matrice 300 drone, equipped with a \qty{20}{\mega\pixel} RGB camera with a focal length of \qty{8.8}{\milli\metre}/\qty{24}{\milli\metre} integrated into the Zenmuse L1 sensor. The RTK positioning system, connected to the ARAGEA regional active geodetic network, was used to obtain the coordinates of each image with centimetre-level accuracy.

The flight mission took half an hour and started at 11:30~a.m. on 17 June 2024. The area was flown over at a constant altitude of \qty{100}{\metre} above the terrain, maintaining a constant height relative to the ground throughout the entire flight, and at a speed of \qty{3}{\metre\per\second}. Both the lateral and forward overlap were set to \qty{80}{\percent}. From the RGB images captured with the camera, an orthophoto was generated using WebODM software (v.3.2.6; \citealp{webodm}), following the default parameters for orthomosaic generation. Coordinate information and other image metadata were extracted from the EXIF data of each image. An orthomosaic with a spatial resolution of \qty{5}{\centi\metre} was obtained, along with the point cloud collected with the Zenmuse L1 LiDAR payload.

\subsection{Shrub instance-segmentation models}

\subsubsection{Dataset preparation}

The deep-learning models were developed using the JuniperMapper dataset described by \citet{khaldi2023junipermapper}. The dataset was originally designed for the individual delineation of medium- and large-sized \textit{Juniperus} shrubs and includes reference annotations derived from both expert photo-interpretation (PI) and field observations.

The PI dataset comprises 712 image tiles of 448 $\times$ 448 pixels, divided into predefined training (570), validation (67) and test (75) subsets. These datasets were employed during the two steps training of the models. First, the model weights were fine-tuned on the training subset. Neither model underwent a hyperparameter search; each used a fixed configuration taken from previous work. Second, a checkpoint selection phase was conducted on the validation subset using the highest mask AP$_{50}$.

Mask AP was computed with the standard dataset-level COCO evaluation. Mask AP$_{50}$ uses an IoU threshold of 0.50, and mask AP$_{50:95}$, which averages AP over IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 in increments of 0.05, was used as a secondary criterion to resolve ties.

Validation mask AP was used only to select checkpoints within each architecture, not to compare architectures. Architectures were compared using the post-training evaluation with the JuniperMapper test set and the field-work (FW) dataset. The FW dataset contains 1,771 field-derived shrub masks across 124 images. It was used to select the confidence threshold of each model (Section~\ref{sec:post_training_ev}) and to assess how the fixed models transferred from photo-interpreted reference data to field-derived shrub delineations.

\subsubsection{Mask R-CNN}

The first model was a Mask R-CNN implemented in Detectron2 and configured following \citet{khaldi2024individual}. The architecture used a ResNet-101 backbone with a C4 feature-extraction head and was initialized from weights pretrained on the COCO dataset.

The model was subsequently fine-tuned end-to-end using the configuration derived from \citet{khaldi2024individual}, including a base learning rate of 0.0025, 4,000 training iterations, and an ROI-head batch size of 256 proposals per image (Table~\ref{tab:maskrcnn_hparams}). Hyperparameters not reported by \citet{khaldi2024individual} were kept at their Detectron2 default values. The model was evaluated on the PI validation subset every two epochs during training.

\subsubsection{SAM 3}

The second model was SAM 3 \citep{carion2026sam3}, which combines prompt-conditioned detection and segmentation with a shared Perception Encoder for visual and language representations.

Two SAM 3 regimes were evaluated. First, the pretrained model was applied in zero-shot mode using the text prompt ``shrub'', without domain-specific training. This experiment provided a baseline for assessing the transferability of the pretrained representation to overhead shrub imagery.

Second, SAM 3 was adapted to the JuniperMapper domain. The vision and language backbones were kept frozen, and only the detection and segmentation components were fine-tuned on the PI training subset, using ``shrub'' as the text prompt. Training was scheduled for 30 epochs with a batch size of 2. The trainable parameters were optimized with AdamW, using a base learning rate of \num{8e-5} with an inverse-square-root schedule, 20 warm-up steps and a final cooldown. Weight decay was 0.1, excluding biases and normalization layers, and gradients were clipped at a norm of 0.1. The original SAM 3 detection and segmentation loss formulation was retained, and its complete configuration is provided in the code repository.

Data processing followed the original SAM 3 fine-tuning configuration. Training images were resized to a random square size of at most 1008 $\times$ 1008 pixels and padded to the model's 1008 $\times$ 1008 pixel input resolution. Validation images were resized directly to 1008 $\times$ 1008 pixels. The model was evaluated on the PI validation subset after each epoch.

\subsubsection{SAM 3 prompt sensitivity analysis}

The text prompt ``shrub'' was defined as the primary SAM 3 prompt before any evaluation. It was the category name used to domain-adapt the SAM 3 decoder and to select the checkpoint on the validation split, and it was used for the main comparison between architectures.

A supplementary prompt-sensitivity analysis then evaluated nine alternative short noun phrases describing shrub vegetation: ``bush'', ``low shrub'', ``small shrub'', ``small bush'', ``woody shrub'', ``Mediterranean shrub'', ``dry shrub'', ``burned shrub'' and ``brown shrub''. To keep the analysis consistent with model development, each prompt was scored with the same data and metric used for checkpoint selection: the JuniperMapper Photo Interpretation validation split (67 images) and COCO mask AP$_{50}$, with the selected checkpoint and all other settings unchanged.

Because the alternative prompts were evaluated on the same validation split used to select the SAM 3 checkpoint with the ``shrub'' prompt, the analysis was treated as exploratory rather than as a post-hoc prompt optimization.

\subsubsection{Computational environment}

Mask R-CNN and SAM 3 were trained and evaluated on a workstation equipped with an Intel Core i7 processor, \qty{64}{\giga\byte} RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 Ti GPU with \qty{16}{\giga\byte} VRAM, under Ubuntu running through WSL2 on Windows 11 with CUDA acceleration.

\subsection{Post-training evaluation}\label{sec:post_training_ev}

After model development, the selected checkpoint from each architecture was evaluated on the PI test and FW datasets. Following \citet{khaldi2024individual}, prediction performance was examined across a range of confidence-score thresholds to characterize the precision-recall trade-off and determine an appropriate operating point for deployment.

Performance was characterized using instance-level mask IoU and the directional S-IoU formulation proposed by \citet{khaldi2024individual}. For conventional IoU, predicted and reference instances were evaluated using their best-matching counterpart, and a detection was considered a true positive when the mask overlap exceeded the specified IoU threshold. IoU thresholds of 0.50 and 0.75 were considered, and the same two values were applied to S-IoU. Hereafter, the matching criterion is written as IoU~=~0.50, IoU~=~0.75, S-IoU~=~0.50 or S-IoU~=~0.75, whereas precision, recall, F1, AP and confidence thresholds are reported as percentages.

The S-IoU was configured by \citet{khaldi2024individual} to account for fragmentation and merging of shrub crowns. The metric evaluates the proportion of shrub areas being detected by the model, dividing the intersection by the ground truth area. Rather than considering only the best-matching instance, S-IoU compares each prediction or reference object with the union of the overlapping instances in the opposite set. Prediction- and reference-directed evaluations were therefore performed separately to determine true positives (TPs), false positives (FPs), and false negatives (FNs).

The Equation~\ref{eq:siou_labls} evaluates the labels and was used to compute the number of TPs and FPs. Otherwise, the Equation~\ref{eq:siou_pred} works for the predictions and identifies the number of FNs. $S_{p_{\mathrm{match}}}$ represents the set of all matching predictions and $S_{l_{\mathrm{match}}}$ all matching labels or reference masks. $l_k$ and $p_k$ are the label and prediction of id $k$, respectively.

\begin{equation}
\text{S-IoU}(p, S_{l_{\mathrm{match}}}) = \frac{
    \mathrm{Area}(p \cap \bigcup_{k=1}^{|S_{l_{\mathrm{match}}}|} l_k)
}{
    \operatorname{Area}(\bigcup_{k=1}^{|S_{l_{\mathrm{match}}}|} l_k)
}
\label{eq:siou_labls}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\text{S-IoU}(l, S_{p_{\mathrm{match}}}) = \frac{
    \mathrm{Area}(l \cap \bigcup_{k=1}^{|S_{p_{\mathrm{match}}}|} p_k)
}{
    \operatorname{Area}(l)
}
\label{eq:siou_pred}
\end{equation}

S-IoU was set to 0 for objects without any overlapping counterpart. Given a matching threshold $\tau$ (0.50 or 0.75), a prediction was counted as a true positive (TP) if $\text{S-IoU}(p, S_{l_{\mathrm{match}}}) \geq \tau$ and as a false positive (FP) otherwise, and a reference mask was counted as a false negative (FN) if $\text{S-IoU}(l, S_{p_{\mathrm{match}}}) < \tau$. Counts were accumulated over all images, and precision, recall and F1-score were calculated as in Equation~\ref{eq:prf1}.

\begin{equation}
    \text{Precision} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}},
    \qquad
    \text{Recall} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}},
    \qquad
    \text{F1} = \frac{2 \cdot \text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}} .
    \label{eq:prf1}
\end{equation}

Because TPs and FNs are determined from different directions, the sum of TPs and FNs need not equal the number of reference masks.

Precision, recall, and F1-score were calculated across prediction-confidence thresholds for both the PI test and FW datasets. Consistent with \citet{khaldi2024individual}, the PI test dataset was used to characterize model performance within the photo-interpretation domain, whereas the independently field-derived FW dataset was used to characterize performance under conditions more representative of real-world deployment.

For each model, F1-score response curves across confidence thresholds were calculated separately for the PI test and FW datasets. The operational confidence threshold for subsequent deployment was selected using the FW dataset, as the field-based reference data provide the closest available approximation to the conditions encountered during large-area application. True positives were defined using S-IoU~=~0.50. The operational point was defined as the highest confidence threshold for which the F1-score was within 1 percentage point of the maximum F1-score obtained on the FW dataset. This criterion favors more confident predictions and reduces false-positive detections while maintaining near-optimal F1 performance. The selected threshold and its corresponding precision, recall, and F1-score were reported together with the full confidence-threshold response curves.

\subsection{Independent study-area validation}

Field validation was conducted in the Encinacorba study area using 26 plots of \qty{9}{\square\metre} distributed along two longitudinal transects. Within each plot, vegetation composition and percentage cover were recorded. Shrub and tree objects were subsequently delineated from the field-reference information and corresponding orthomosaic imagery, producing 58 shrub polygons and 21 tree polygons (Figure~\ref{label_examples}).

The 58 shrubs contain 46 \textit{L. stoechas}, 8 \textit{C. laurifolius}, 4 \textit{Erica vagans} L.; and the 21 trees contain 13 \textit{Q. ilex}, 6 \textit{P. pinaster}, and 2 \textit{C. monogyna}.

\subsection{Orthomosaic inference and mask reconstruction}

Each selected model was deployed over the orthomosaic using overlapping tiles of 448 $\times$ 448 pixels with a 64-pixel overlap, matching the size of the JuniperMapper images used for training. The same tiling was used for both models. SAM 3 internally resizes every tile to its fixed 1008 $\times$ 1008 pixel input resolution, which preserves the aspect ratio of the square tiles and keeps shrubs at the scale used for checkpoint selection. Tiles were passed to the models as unmodified 8-bit RGB, and pixels outside the orthomosaic footprint were excluded using its alpha band. Predicted instance masks were converted to georeferenced polygons. Instances smaller than 4 pixels (\qty{0.01}{\square\metre}) were discarded.

To reduce tile-boundary fragmentation, polygons from different inference windows that intersected, touched or were separated by no more than \qty{0.05}{\metre} were grouped and unioned, and small gaps between the merged parts were closed. Polygons from the same window were not merged. The maximum, mean and median confidence scores of each group were retained.

The resulting shrub maps were compared with the field reference. Because most reference polygons extended beyond the plots, no geometry was clipped. Each reference polygon was matched against all predictions, and a matched prediction was counted as a true positive wherever it lay. Since all shrubs within each plot were digitized, an unmatched prediction was counted as a false positive only when at least half of its area lay inside a plot; outside the plots, it could correspond to an undigitized shrub.

Predicted shrubs were matched to reference shrub polygons using greedy one-to-one matching with an IoU threshold of 0.10. Precision was calculated as the proportion of true positives among true and false positives, recall as the proportion of reference shrubs successfully matched, and F1 as the harmonic mean of precision and recall.

Tree commission was calculated as the proportion of reference tree polygons matched by a prediction, using the same one-to-one matching (IoU $\geq$ 0.10) applied separately to the tree polygons. It describes where part of the false positives fell and is not a separate test.

\subsection{LiDAR processing}

The UAV LiDAR point cloud was initially pre-processed using DJI Terra (v.5.3.5) software. Owing to data-storage constraints and to reduce the computational requirements of subsequent processing steps, a subset corresponding to approximately one-sixth of the original point cloud was retained for further analysis. The remaining processing was conducted in R Statistical Software (v4.6.1; \citealp{rsoftware}) using the lidR R package (v.4.3.2; \citealp{lidr2020}, \citealp{lidr2026}). All the scripts to conduct the following processing operations are inside the \textit{R} folder in the repository.

First, Statistical Outlier Removal (SOR) operation was applied using 10 nearest neighbors and a three-standard-deviation rejection threshold. Then, a ground classification was performed. Three ground-classification algorithms were evaluated: Multiscale Curvature Classification (MCC), Progressive Morphological Filtering (PMF), and Progressive TIN Densification (PTD). Ground-classification performance was assessed qualitatively using elevation profiles along four representative transects and quantitatively against surveyed GNSS elevations. The last dataset was collected by a Leica GNSS receiver in the center of each plot with a centimetric accuracy.

For quantitative assessment, LiDAR-derived digital terrain models (DTMs) for each classification method of each GNSS reference point were extracted. Each DTM was built with a TIN-interpolated method from each classified point cloud on the \qty{0.05}{\metre} orthomosaic grid. Each DTM was then sampled with bilinear interpolation at the GNSS targets. Elevation residuals were calculated relative to the surveyed orthometric elevation, and the mean RMSE across the GNSS targets was used to select the ground-classification method subsequently used for point-cloud normalization. The 26 validation areas were covered by all DTMs, so every method was evaluated on the same set of targets.

The Canopy Height Model (CHM) was produced in two steps from the most accurate ground-classified point cloud. First, the point cloud was height-normalized by subtracting from each point the ground elevation interpolated at its location from the classified ground returns with k-nearest-neighbor inverse distance weighting (10 neighbors, power of 2, maximum search radius of \qty{50}{\metre}). Second, the normalized point cloud was rasterized into a CHM by assigning to each cell the highest normalized return within it. The CHM grid had a spatial resolution of \qty{0.25}{\metre} and was obtained by aggregating the orthomosaic grid by an integer factor, so the two remained aligned. This resolution was chosen because at \qty{5}{\centi\metre} only \qty{5.5}{\percent} of the cells contained a direct LiDAR return, compared with \qty{69}{\percent} at \qty{0.25}{\metre}. Cells without returns were retained as NoData rather than interpolated.

\subsection{LiDAR-based refinement}

The CHM was used as a post-processing step to refine the shrub maps. It was not provided as input to either deep-learning model; therefore, the same refinement procedure could be applied independently to the predictions generated by both architectures. Because CHM heights close to ground level were within the range of LiDAR noise, the CHM was used only to distinguish trees from shrubs. Predictions dominated by bare ground were not filtered and were instead left to the model confidence threshold.

The refinement procedure consisted of the following steps. First, CHM cells whose centers fell within each predicted polygon were extracted. The number of valid cells, coverage (i.e., the proportion of cells with a valid height), mean height, maximum height, 90th-percentile height (P90), and tall-cell fraction ($f$) were then calculated. The tall-cell fraction was defined as the proportion of valid cells exceeding a height threshold $h$.

Each prediction was subsequently assigned to one of four post-processing categories, evaluated in the following order:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Insufficient support.} Predictions with coverage below 0.3 or fewer than five valid cells were retained without modification. Predictions smaller than one CHM cell or located outside the CHM extent had no valid cells and therefore fell into this category.

\item \textbf{Rejected.} Predictions with P90 $> h$ and a tall-cell fraction $\geq f$ were removed as trees.

\item \textbf{Clipped.} Predictions containing at least one cell above $h$ but not meeting the rejection criterion had their tall cells removed, after which the largest remaining connected component was retained. If this component was smaller than \qty{0.25}{\square\metre}, the prediction was rejected.

\item \textbf{Unchanged.} All remaining predictions were retained without modification.
\end{itemize}

The thresholds $h$ and $f$ were calibrated using the 79 vegetation masks from the study area, comprising 58 shrubs and 21 trees. The growth form (tree or shrub) of each mask was assigned according to the species identified in the field. Masks representing \textit{Q. ilex}, \textit{P. pinaster}, and \textit{C. monogyna} were classified as trees, whereas the remaining masks were classified as shrubs.

All combinations of $h \in \{1.3, 1.5, 1.7, 1.9\}$~\unit{\metre} and $f \in \{0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5\}$ were evaluated using the procedure described above. The selection rule was defined prior to calibration. Among the parameter combinations that neither rejected nor clipped any shrub mask, the combination that rejected the largest number of tree masks was selected. Ties were resolved in favor of the higher value of $h$, followed by the higher value of $f$.

Because the thresholds were selected using the same masks on which their performance was assessed, the selection procedure was repeated using a leave-one-ROI-out approach. Each mask was assigned to the \qty{9}{\square\metre} field ROI with which it had the greatest spatial overlap, or to the nearest ROI when no overlap occurred (three masks, with a maximum distance of \qty{0.85}{\metre}). For each of the 26 ROIs, the thresholds were selected using masks from the remaining ROIs and then applied to the masks belonging to the held-out ROI.

The selected parameters were subsequently applied without modification to the predictions generated by both architectures. Performance before and after LiDAR-based refinement was compared using the same independent study-area validation procedure.

\subsection{Evaluation on held-out JuniperMapper data}

After model selection, the fixed Mask R-CNN and domain-adapted SAM 3 models were evaluated on the FW and PI test datasets. The FW results are presented first because the deployment operating points were selected on them.

\subsubsection{Field-work dataset performance}

On the FW dataset, the two architectures responded very differently to the prediction-confidence threshold (Figure~\ref{fig:internal_performance_fw}). At IoU~=~0.50 and S-IoU~=~0.50, Mask R-CNN performance increased steeply from F1 values of \qty{15}{\percent} (IoU) and \qty{17}{\percent} (S-IoU) at \qty{0}{\percent} confidence, where all 31,744 candidate predictions were retained, to a broad plateau. F1 peaked at \qty{35}{\percent} confidence (IoU: \qty{70.4}{\percent}; S-IoU: \qty{75.8}{\percent}). S-IoU-based F1 remained within 1 percentage point of this maximum between \qty{20}{\percent} and \qty{45}{\percent}. Above \qty{60}{\percent} confidence, F1 declined gradually, with a steeper decrease above \qty{85}{\percent}, reaching approximately \qty{46}{\percent} at \qty{95}{\percent}.

In contrast, SAM 3 showed a unimodal, sharply peaked response. F1 increased from \qty{22}{\percent} at \qty{0}{\percent} confidence, where the large number of low-scoring predictions reduced precision, to a maximum at \qtyrange{55}{60}{\percent} confidence (IoU: \qty{74.9}{\percent}; S-IoU: \qty{78.3}{\percent}). It then decreased to approximately \qty{60}{\percent} at \qty{80}{\percent} confidence and \qty{5}{\percent} at \qty{95}{\percent}, because few predictions received scores above \qty{85}{\percent}. Mask R-CNN was therefore substantially less sensitive to threshold selection than SAM 3. On the S-IoU~=~0.50 curve, increasing the threshold by 20 percentage points above the optimum reduced F1 by only 2 points for Mask R-CNN (from \qty{75.8}{\percent} at \qty{35}{\percent} to \qty{73.8}{\percent} at \qty{55}{\percent}), but by approximately 18 points for SAM 3 (from \qty{78.3}{\percent} at \qty{60}{\percent} to \qty{60.5}{\percent} at \qty{80}{\percent}).

\subsection{Calibration of the LiDAR refinement rule}

CHM measurements were available for all 79 field-reference masks. The median number of valid CHM cells per mask was 15 for shrubs and 96 for trees. Under the minimum-support criteria, 10 shrub masks, all corresponding to small \textit{L. stoechas} individuals, had insufficient LiDAR support and would therefore be retained without modification. No tree mask fell into this category.

Mean CHM height differed clearly between growth forms (Figure~\ref{fig:species_mean_height}). \textit{Q. ilex} (\qty{2.87}{\metre}) and \textit{P. pinaster} (\qty{2.45}{\metre}) were the tallest species, followed by \textit{C. monogyna} (\qty{1.00}{\metre}). The mean height of \textit{C. monogyna} was closer to that of the tallest shrub species, \textit{C. laurifolius} (\qty{0.67}{\metre}), than to the other tree species. \textit{E. vagans} (\qty{0.21}{\metre}) and \textit{L. stoechas} (\qty{0.13}{\metre}) had the lowest mean heights.

Species-level means nevertheless concealed substantial overlap at the individual-mask level. Six tree masks had mean heights below \qty{1}{\metre}: three \textit{Q. ilex}, two \textit{P. pinaster}, and one \textit{C. monogyna}. Similar values were reached by \textit{C. laurifolius} masks, whose mean heights extended up to \qty{1.03}{\metre}. P90 ranged from 0 to \qty{1.46}{\metre} in shrub masks and from 1.35 to \qty{6.75}{\metre} in tree masks. No CHM cell within any shrub mask exceeded \qty{1.63}{\metre}. The CHM also contained slightly negative values, reaching \qty{-0.05}{\metre}, which were within the expected near-ground noise range and were close in magnitude to the mean height of \textit{L. stoechas} (\qty{0.13}{\metre}).

P90 was preferred over maximum height because it was less sensitive to isolated high returns. For example, two \textit{L. stoechas} masks had maximum heights of 1.52 and \qty{1.54}{\metre}, whereas their P90 values were only 0.22 and \qty{0.33}{\metre}, respectively.

Consequently, these individuals could not be separated from shrubs using CHM height alone. \textit{C. monogyna} can also occur as a tall shrub, further limiting its separation based solely on height. The four clipped trees had P90 values between 1.55 and \qty{1.69}{\metre}.

The minimum-coverage criterion had only a limited influence on threshold selection. Increasing the minimum coverage from 0.3 to 0.5 would have classified two additional \textit{P. pinaster} masks, with coverage values of 0.44 and 0.48, as having insufficient support. Under this criterion, the selected parameter combination would have rejected 13 trees instead of 14.

In the leave-one-ROI-out procedure, the selection rule chose \qty{1.7}{\metre} with a tall-cell fraction of 0.1 in 25 of the 26 folds. In the remaining fold, exclusion of one tree made thresholds of 1.7 and \qty{1.9}{\metre} equivalent on the training masks, and \qty{1.9}{\metre} was selected according to the predefined tie-breaking rule. Pooled across the held-out folds, 13 of 21 trees were rejected, 4 were clipped, and 4 were retained, while no shrub was rejected or clipped. Thus, leave-one-ROI-out validation reduced tree rejection by only one mask relative to the full calibration sample while preserving the absence of shrub modification.

As a sensitivity analysis, the selection procedure was repeated while allowing shrub clipping. Under this alternative criterion, \qty{1.5}{\metre} was selected in 25 folds. However, when the ROI containing the tallest \textit{C. laurifolius} mask was held out, the selected threshold decreased to \qty{1.3}{\metre}, causing that shrub to be rejected when evaluated as the held-out observation. This result suggests that requiring zero shrub modification during calibration favored a more conservative threshold that was more robust to unseen shrub masks. In particular, the selected threshold of \qty{1.7}{\metre} remained above the maximum shrub-cell height observed in the calibration sample (\qty{1.63}{\metre}).

Each model was deployed over the Encinacorba orthomosaic at its FW operating point (\qty{45}{\percent} for Mask R-CNN and \qty{60}{\percent} for SAM 3). After the tile predictions were merged, the Mask R-CNN map contained 1,726 shrub polygons covering \qty{7909}{\square\metre}, whereas the SAM 3 map contained 3,377 polygons covering \qty{15700}{\square\metre}. The median polygon area was \qty{2.5}{\square\metre} for Mask R-CNN and \qty{3.1}{\square\metre} for SAM 3. At the map scale, SAM 3 therefore produced approximately twice as many predicted objects and twice the mapped area as Mask R-CNN.

\subsection{Cross-domain validation in the study area}

Both models transferred poorly to the field reference of the study area (Table~\ref{tab:field_validation}). Mask R-CNN matched 1 of the 58 reference shrubs (recall \qty{1.7}{\percent}), whereas SAM 3 matched 2 (recall \qty{3.4}{\percent}). F1 was \qty{3.2}{\percent} for Mask R-CNN and \qty{6.1}{\percent} for SAM 3, compared with \qty{75.2}{\percent} and \qty{78.3}{\percent}, respectively, at the same operating points on the JuniperMapper FW dataset.

These two sets of values are not directly equivalent metrics. The JuniperMapper results were obtained using S-IoU~=~0.50, which scores each instance against the union of all overlapping counterparts, whereas the study-area validation used greedy one-to-one matching with IoU~$\geq$~0.10 against a small, plot-based field reference. The difference should therefore not be interpreted as a simple domain-transfer decrease of approximately 72 percentage points. Nevertheless, the study-area results show that both deployed models recovered almost none of the reference shrubs, even under the more lenient overlap requirement.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Model performance and transferability within the JuniperMapper domain}

The Mask R-CNN implementation reproduced the performance reported by \citet{khaldi2024individual} reasonably well, despite differences in the confidence-score distributions between the two training runs. On the PI test set, the maximum F1 scores differed by less than 1.7 percentage points across the four evaluation criteria, while somewhat larger differences were observed on the FW dataset. More importantly, the overall response to the confidence threshold was similar in both studies: performance on the PI data peaked at relatively high confidence values, whereas the FW data produced a broader optimum at lower confidence values. This consistency indicates that the reproduced model retained the general behavior of the original implementation even though its confidence scores were not identically calibrated.

The differences in confidence scores are relevant for model deployment. The Mask R-CNN trained here retained fewer predictions than the model reported by \citet{khaldi2024individual} when evaluated at the thresholds used in the original study, resulting in higher precision but lower recall. Its optimal FW threshold was consequently lower. This demonstrates that confidence thresholds should be calibrated for each trained model rather than transferred directly from previous experiments, even when the architecture, training dataset, and principal hyperparameters are shared. The same consideration applies when comparing different architectures because confidence scores from Mask R-CNN and SAM 3 are not calibrated on a common scale. The use of architecture-specific operating points in this study therefore represents the intended operating behavior of each model rather than a direct comparison of equal numerical confidence values.

Within the JuniperMapper datasets, domain-adapted SAM 3 consistently performed better than the Mask R-CNN reproduction. The improvement was modest at the 0.50 matching threshold but became more evident for IoU~=~0.75, where accurate delineation of individual crowns is required. This suggests that the advantage of SAM 3 was not limited to detecting a larger number of shrubs but also involved more accurate spatial delineation. Nevertheless, the difference between the two architectures was comparatively small relative to the much larger performance decline observed when either model was transferred to the independent study area.

The comparison between IoU and S-IoU provides additional information on the type of segmentation errors. S-IoU-based F1 was consistently higher than conventional IoU-based F1 because S-IoU accommodates fragmented and merged crowns by evaluating an instance against the union of its overlapping counterparts. The larger difference between these metrics on the FW dataset than on the PI test set indicates that crown fragmentation, merging, or boundary disagreement became more important when the models were evaluated against field-derived annotations. In Mask R-CNN, IoU-based performance also tended to improve with increasing prediction confidence, suggesting that highly confident predictions generally had more accurate crown boundaries. These results reinforce the value of reporting both metrics because conventional IoU alone can penalize geometrically plausible predictions when individual shrub crowns are difficult to separate.

The SAM 3 prompt-sensitivity experiment further showed that the domain-adapted model was relatively insensitive to closely related category names. Generic descriptions such as ``shrub'', ``bush'', ``low shrub'', and ``Mediterranean shrub'' produced very similar validation performance, whereas prompts including condition or color descriptors performed worse. Thus, after domain adaptation, the exact synonym used to describe the target appears less important than avoiding unnecessarily restrictive semantic modifiers. Because these alternatives were evaluated on the same validation data used during model development, however, these differences should be interpreted as exploratory rather than as evidence for an optimized prompt.

\subsection{Cross-domain transfer to post-fire Mediterranean shrubland}

The main limitation identified in this study was the poor transfer of both RGB-based models from JuniperMapper to the Encinacorba study area. Although both architectures achieved high performance on the JuniperMapper PI and FW datasets, they recovered very few of the 58 field-reference shrubs in Encinacorba. These results cannot be converted directly into a numerical domain-transfer loss because the JuniperMapper and study-area evaluations used different matching procedures. Nevertheless, the extremely low recall in Encinacorba, despite the lenient IoU threshold used for validation, demonstrates that the principal problem was not the choice of evaluation metric but the inability of the transferred models to detect the shrub community present in the new environment.

The discrepancy is consistent with a substantial domain shift between the training and deployment vegetation. JuniperMapper was developed for the delineation of medium- and large-sized \textit{Juniperus} individuals, whereas the post-fire community at Encinacorba contains abundant low-stature species, particularly \textit{L. stoechas}, together with bare soil, necromass, grasses, shadows, and structurally heterogeneous woody vegetation. Visual inspection showed that the models preferentially detected taller and more conspicuous vegetation while many small \textit{L. stoechas} individuals were missed. Similar difficulties have been reported for low-stature shrub communities with patchy canopies and mixtures of co-occurring vegetation \citep{uyeda2020vegetation}. The results therefore indicate that transfer learning from a morphologically different shrub dataset was insufficient to bridge this ecological and structural difference.

This finding also places the comparison between Mask R-CNN and SAM 3 in context. SAM 3 clearly performed better within the JuniperMapper domain and generated approximately twice as many polygons and twice the mapped shrub area when deployed over Encinacorba. However, neither architecture achieved adequate recall against the independent field reference. Furthermore, precision estimates in the study area were based on only four scored Mask R-CNN predictions and eight SAM 3 predictions and are therefore too unstable to support a reliable ranking between architectures. The apparent \qty{100}{\percent} precision of Mask R-CNN after LiDAR refinement is particularly misleading because it was based on a single retained prediction that predominantly corresponded to a \textit{C. monogyna} tree and overlapped a small \textit{L. stoechas} reference polygon only marginally. Consequently, the independent validation provides stronger evidence of a general transferability limitation than of a meaningful performance difference between the two RGB models.

The qualitative errors support the same interpretation. Shadowed areas were occasionally detected as shrubs by both models, illustrating the difficulty of separating vegetation from spectrally similar image features using RGB information alone. Mask R-CNN additionally produced some straight mask boundaries aligned with inference-tile edges. Comparable artifacts were not evident in the SAM 3 output, potentially reflecting differences in the spatial representation of the two architectures and their response to overlapping inference windows. However, these boundary effects were secondary to the much larger problem of missing low shrubs.

\subsection{Contribution and limitations of LiDAR refinement}

LiDAR provided useful structural information for reducing one specific source of commission error: the classification of trees as shrubs. The selected CHM refinement reduced tree commission from \qty{28.6}{\percent} to \qty{4.8}{\percent} for Mask R-CNN and from \qty{61.9}{\percent} to \qty{14.3}{\percent} for SAM 3 without removing any of the shrub predictions that had been matched before refinement. At the map scale, it rejected a large proportion of the originally predicted area, particularly for SAM 3, showing that many RGB predictions corresponded to relatively tall vegetation rather than to the low shrub layer of interest.

The conservative calibration strategy was important for this result. A threshold of \qty{1.7}{\metre} with a tall-cell fraction of 0.1 was selected because it removed a substantial proportion of tree masks without modifying any shrub reference mask. The leave-one-ROI-out analysis produced almost the same threshold and retained the absence of shrub modification, indicating that the rule was relatively stable within the available reference sample. By contrast, allowing shrub clipping during calibration favored lower height thresholds and caused an unseen shrub to be rejected in one held-out fold. Prioritizing shrub preservation was therefore appropriate for a refinement intended to remove obvious tree commission rather than to redefine shrub geometry.

However, vegetation height alone could not completely separate the two growth forms. Several tree masks had P90 heights comparable to those of the tallest \textit{C. laurifolius} shrubs, and some of these low tree masks were also responsible for errors that remained after refinement. These individuals may represent low-stature trees or post-fire resprouts, for which growth form cannot be inferred reliably from a single CHM threshold. Increasing the strictness of the height filter would consequently remove additional trees only at the cost of beginning to modify genuine shrubs.

The accuracy of the CHM itself also constrains the refinement. All evaluated ground-classification methods showed a positive vertical bias relative to the GNSS measurements, with PMF producing the lowest RMSE and therefore being selected for normalization. The consistent positive residuals are compatible with the difficulty of identifying the true soil surface beneath dense low vegetation, litter, and tree crowns, where the lowest available LiDAR return may still occur above ground. This limitation was particularly evident beneath dense tree crowns. Although part of the residual difference could also reflect a systematic offset between the GNSS and LiDAR vertical references, the present design cannot separate these effects.

The \qty{0.25}{\metre} CHM resolution provided a compromise between spatial detail and LiDAR-return density, but support remained limited for the smallest shrubs. Consequently, heights near ground level could not be used reliably to identify low shrubs. The CHM was therefore deliberately restricted to separating sufficiently tall vegetation from predicted shrubs rather than being used as an independent shrub detector. This distinction is important: LiDAR refinement can remove structurally implausible RGB predictions, but it cannot recover shrubs that were not detected by the segmentation model in the first place. Accordingly, LiDAR substantially reduced tree commission but produced only marginal changes in F1 because recall remained unchanged.

\subsection{Implications for post-fire shrub mapping}

Taken together, the results show that the principal bottleneck in this workflow is not post-processing but cross-domain shrub detection. Both deep-learning architectures performed well when evaluated on data representative of their training domain, and SAM 3 provided a modest but consistent improvement over Mask R-CNN within JuniperMapper. However, these advantages did not translate into reliable delineation of the structurally different post-fire shrub community at Encinacorba. The failure was particularly pronounced for small, low-stature shrubs, which cannot subsequently be recovered through height-based filtering.

For applications requiring shrub mapping across heterogeneous Mediterranean environments, these findings argue against assuming that transfer learning from a single shrub morphology is sufficient. A more representative training dataset should include the range of species, crown sizes, vegetation densities, background conditions, and post-fire growth forms expected during deployment. Fine-tuning with locally annotated examples from the target ecosystem may therefore be more important than further adjustment of confidence or CHM thresholds. The strong contrast between performance on JuniperMapper and Encinacorba also emphasizes the importance of independent field validation: high performance on a held-out subset of the source dataset, or even on field-derived annotations from the same general domain, does not guarantee successful geographical and ecological transfer.

Structural information nevertheless remains valuable as a complementary source. In this study, LiDAR effectively identified a large component of tree-related commission while preserving detected shrubs, demonstrating that RGB segmentation and CHM-based filtering address different error mechanisms. A future workflow could exploit LiDAR information more directly during model training or multimodal inference rather than only as a post-processing rule. Such integration may help distinguish woody growth forms that are spectrally similar in RGB imagery, although improved representation of low shrubs in the training data remains necessary.

Finally, the independent validation sample was relatively small and plot based, and several accuracy estimates were derived from very few matched predictions. These values should therefore be interpreted primarily as evidence of poor cross-domain transfer rather than as precise estimates of landscape-scale accuracy or as a basis for ranking the two architectures. Broader field validation covering additional shrub communities, topographic conditions, and post-fire structures would be required to determine how general the observed transferability limitations are.

\section{Conclusions}

This study evaluated the transferability of Mask R-CNN and SAM 3 for individual shrub mapping from very-high-resolution UAV RGB imagery and assessed whether UAV LiDAR-derived structural information could improve the resulting maps. The results demonstrate a clear contrast between performance within the source domain and performance under independent ecological transfer.

Both architectures performed well on the JuniperMapper datasets, confirming that deep-learning models can accurately delineate shrubs when training and evaluation data represent similar vegetation structures. The reproduced Mask R-CNN achieved performance comparable to that reported in the original JuniperMapper study, while the domain-adapted SAM 3 model produced consistently higher F1-scores, particularly under the stricter IoU~=~0.75 criterion. SAM 3 therefore showed an advantage in individual crown delineation within the source domain.

This advantage did not translate into successful mapping of the post-fire shrub community in Encinacorba. Both models recovered very few of the field-reference shrubs, with recall values of only \qty{1.7}{\percent} for Mask R-CNN and \qty{3.4}{\percent} for SAM 3. The dominant limitation was the failure to detect small and low-stature vegetation, particularly \textit{Lavandula stoechas}, rather than insufficient post-processing of otherwise correct predictions. The large decrease in performance indicates that transfer learning from shrubs with substantially different crown structures was insufficient to overcome the ecological and structural domain shift between JuniperMapper and the target Mediterranean post-fire environment.

The LiDAR-based refinement successfully addressed a different component of the error. Using a conservatively calibrated CHM threshold substantially reduced the number of trees incorrectly classified as shrubs while preserving all previously matched shrub predictions. Tree commission decreased from \qty{28.6}{\percent} to \qty{4.8}{\percent} for Mask R-CNN and from \qty{61.9}{\percent} to \qty{14.3}{\percent} for SAM 3. However, because the refinement could only remove existing predictions and not detect missed shrubs, its effect on overall F1 was limited. Height information alone was also unable to distinguish some low-stature trees from the tallest shrubs because their structural distributions overlapped.

The principal limitation of the proposed workflow is therefore the generalization of the RGB segmentation models rather than the LiDAR refinement stage. For operational mapping of heterogeneous Mediterranean shrublands, training data should represent the range of species, crown sizes, vegetation densities, and post-fire growth forms expected in the target landscape. Fine-tuning with locally annotated imagery is likely to provide greater benefits than further adjustment of confidence thresholds or CHM filtering rules.

Overall, SAM 3 provided the strongest segmentation performance within the training domain, whereas UAV LiDAR proved useful for reducing tree-related commission errors after deployment. Their combination nevertheless remained constrained by poor detection of structurally dissimilar shrub vegetation. The study therefore highlights the importance of independent ecological validation and representative training data when transferring deep-learning vegetation-mapping models across landscapes.
