\section{Introduction}

Accurate characterization of forest cover is essential for sustainable ecosystem management, land-use planning, ecological research, and the design of restoration and risk-prevention measures \citep{chazdon2008beyond, townshend2012global}. In Mediterranean ecosystems, shrublands are also particularly important because they protect soils from erosion, support biodiversity, contribute to vegetation succession, and influence fuel accumulation and wildfire behavior \citep{keeley2005determinants}.

Mediterranean shrublands consist mainly of low, densely branched woody vegetation adapted to long, dry summers, mild winters, and recurrent fire. Many shrub species recover rapidly after fire through vegetative resprouting or fire-stimulated germination, making shrubland development an important indicator of post-fire regeneration \citep{keeley1986resilience,pausas2008wildfires}. In fire-affected areas, accurately mapping shrub vegetation is therefore necessary to assess ecological recovery, estimate biomass accumulation, evaluate future fire risk, and plan restoration and forest-management actions. However, shrubland identification remains challenging because of its high spatial and spectral heterogeneity and its spectral similarity to other land-cover types \citep{liu2026underestimated,west2025landsat}.

Remote sensing has substantially improved the detection and characterization of vegetation by enabling observations over large areas at high spatial, spectral, and temporal resolutions. Traditional land-cover mapping has relied on visual interpretation, spectral classification, and manual digitization, as in regional programs such as CORINE \citep{grekousis2015overview}. Object-Based Image Analysis has also been applied by classifying imagery into homogeneous regions and assigning classes according to spectral, textural, and spatial properties \citep{blaschke2010object,soubry2022mapping}. Although these approaches can produce reliable maps, their performance depends heavily on operator expertise, parameter selection, and analysis scale, and they may have difficulty representing structurally complex and heterogeneous vegetation \citep{mishra2014mapping}.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning (DL), have created new opportunities for overcoming these limitations. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) can automatically extract hierarchical and multiscale features from imagery and have improved the classification and segmentation of vegetation and land-cover categories in complex environments \citep{abdi2020land,kattenborn2021review}. Architectures such as U-Net, Mask R-CNN, and DeepLab have been successfully used to delineate forest cover, shrubland, and individual tree structures from high- and very-high-resolution imagery \citep{he2023improvements,guirado2017deeplearning}.

Despite these advances, the automatic delineation of heterogeneous shrub vegetation in post-fire environments remains insufficiently explored. Addressing this gap could improve the efficiency and reliability of post-fire vegetation monitoring while reducing the time requirements and subjectivity associated with manual interpretation. However, the application of CNNs relies on substantial amounts of labeled training data, which are difficult to obtain, particularly in highly diverse ecosystems and in scenarios involving polymorphic shrubs, such as those found in post-fire environments. These conditions increase the difficulty of plant delineation in highly diverse ecosystems \citep{zhang2020identifying}, particularly for polymorphic vegetation where crown overlap and splitting may occur \citep{dong2019progressive}.

To alleviate this limitation, transfer-learning strategies can increase the applicability of CNNs by reusing features learned from previously analyzed datasets \citep{tan2018survey}. This approach reduces both computational requirements and the amount of labeled training data required, which is particularly valuable in environmental applications where reference data are often limited.

Previous studies have also shown that combining spectral, geometric, photogrammetric, and LiDAR-derived information can improve shrub and tree classification. For example, \citet{carbonellrivera2022classification} reported an overall accuracy of approximately 96~\% when classifying Mediterranean shrub and tree species using UAV-derived point clouds and supervised classifiers. Other studies have obtained promising results through multispectral classification, object-based analysis, and rule-based methods incorporating vegetation-height information \citep{uyeda2020vegetation,zahidi2015object}.

The aim of this study is to assess the effectiveness of very-high-spatial-resolution RGB imagery for the automated mapping of shrub cover in a fire-affected forest area. The JuniperMapper dataset \citep{khaldi2023junipermapper} is used to fine-tune the models and assessing their performance in mediterranean shrub mapping. The study comprises one Mask R-CNN implementation and one promptable transformer-based segmentation model. The Detectron2 implementation of Mask R-CNN reproduced the configuration used in the original JuniperMapper study \citep{khaldi2024individual}, providing a literature-based reference for comparison. The SAM 3 promptable model \citep{carion2026SAM 3} is also investigated. The classifications generated by the RGB-based models are combined with UAV LiDAR-derived point-cloud data to determine whether the integration of structural information improves shrub-mapping accuracy.

\section{Methodology}

The workflow of this research is explained in Figure~\ref{fig:methods}. First, the data is collected in the field, i.e., the drone flight and the shrub masks locations to validate the models. Then the data is processed, and the shrub masks digitized. The models were trained and internally evaluated with the JuniperMapper dataset. Finally, the best configuration of each model is deployed in the study area and the metrics were computed using ground reference data. After the creation of the shrub maps, the refinement operation was applied to try to improve the masks and the accuracy metrics were recomputed. The code to reproduce the workflow is located inside the following github repository: \href{https\://github.com/CristianICS/shrub-segmentation}{https\://github.com/CristianICS/shrub-segmentation}.

\subsection{Study area}

The study area is located within the Sierra de Algairén (Figure~\ref{fig:location}), which reaches elevations of approximately 1,000 metres above sea level and is characterised by a Mediterranean climate with continental influences. The climate is characterised by cold winters and hot, dry summers, with marked temperature fluctuations throughout the year. The mean annual temperature is approximately \qty{12}{\celsius} , with winter minima that can fall to \qty{\-2}{\celsius} and summer maxima frequently exceeding \qty{30}{\celsius}. Mean annual precipitation ranges between 500 and 600~mm and is concentrated mainly in spring and autumn. This precipitation regime, together with the dryness of the summer months, promotes soil desiccation and increases the risk of forest fires.

The area selected for model validation is located in the municipality of Encinacorba, Zaragoza (Aragón), within the area affected by the wildfire that occurred on 29 July 2003. A total area of \qty{267.4}{\hectare} was burned. More specifically, the study area comprises \qty{13.3}{\hectare} with a south-facing aspect that was completely burned during the wildfire (Figure 2). Its steep mean slope gradient of 21.5 degrees, has hindered post-fire vegetation recovery.

At present, the study area is dominated by low- to medium-stature shrubland. The most abundant species include \textit{Thymus vulgaris} L., \textit{Sedum sediforme} Jacq., \textit{Lavandula stoechas} L., and \textit{Cistus laurifolius} L. Scattered woody vegetation is also present, including \textit{Quercus ilex} L., \textit{Pinus pinaster} Aiton, and \textit{Crataegus monogyna} Jacq. A high proportion of bare soil and necromass remains.

\subsection{Data acquisition}

The study area was flown with a DJI Matrice 300 drone, equipped with a 20 MP RGB camera with a focal length of 8.8/24~mm integrated into the Zenmuse L1 sensor. The RTK positioning system, connected to the ARAGEA regional active geodetic network, was used to obtain the coordinates of each image with centimetre-level accuracy.

The flight mission took half an hour and started at 11:30~a.m. on 17 June 2024. The area was flown over at a constant altitude of 100 metres above the terrain, maintaining a constant height relative to the ground throughout the entire flight, and at a speed of 3 metres per second. Both the lateral and forward overlap were set to 80 percent. From the RGB images captured with the camera, an orthophoto was generated using WebODM software (v.3.2.6) \citep{webodm}, following the default parameters for orthomosaic generation. Coordinate information and other image metadata were extracted from the EXIF data of each image. An orthomosaic with a spatial resolution of 5~cm was obtained.

In addition, the point cloud collected with the Zenmuse L1 LiDAR payload was initially pre-processed using DJI Terra (v.5.3.5) software. Owing to data-storage constraints and to reduce the computational requirements of subsequent processing steps, a subset corresponding to approximately one-sixth of the original point cloud was retained for further analysis.

\subsection{Shrub instance-segmentation models}

\subsubsection{Dataset preparation and experimental splits}

The deep-learning models were developed using the JuniperMapper dataset described by \citet{khaldi2023junipermapper}. The dataset was originally designed for the individual delineation of medium- and large-sized Juniperus shrubs and includes reference annotations derived from both expert photo-interpretation (PI) and field observations.

The PI dataset comprises 712 image tiles of 448 pixels size, divided into predefined training (570), validation (67) and test (75) subsets. Both models were developed in two steps. First, the model weights were fine-tuned on the training subset: end to end from COCO-pretrained weights for Detectron2, and with the vision and language backbones frozen for SAM 3. Neither model underwent a hyperparameter search; each used a fixed configuration taken from previous work. Second, the checkpoint with the highest mask AP50 on the validation subset was retained. The validation subset was used only for this checkpoint selection, and the PI test subset was withheld until the checkpoint of each model had been fixed.

The JuniperMapper field-work (FW) dataset contains 1,771 field-derived shrub masks across 124 images. It was not used for training or checkpoint selection. It was used to select the confidence threshold of each model (Section X) and to assess how the fixed models transferred from photo-interpreted reference data to field-derived shrub delineations.

Mask AP was computed with the standard dataset-level COCO evaluation for both models. Mask AP averaged over IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 was used as a secondary criterion to resolve ties in validation AP50. Validation AP was used only to select checkpoints within each architecture, not to compare architectures. Architectures were compared using the post-training evaluation described below.

\subsubsection{Detectron2 Mask R-CNN}

The first model was a Mask R-CNN implemented in Detectron2 and configured following \citet{khaldi2024individual}. The architecture used a ResNet-101 backbone with a C4 feature-extraction head and was initialized from weights pretrained on the COCO dataset.

The original JuniperMapper annotations were converted so that each shrub polygon represented an independent COCO instance. The model was subsequently fine-tuned end-to-end using the configuration derived from \citet{khaldi2024individual}, including a base learning rate of 0.0025, 4,000 training iterations, and an ROI-head batch size of 256 proposals per image (Table~\ref{tab:detectron2_hparams}). Hyperparameters not reported by \citet{khaldi2024individual} were kept at their Detectron2 default values.

The model was evaluated on the PI validation subset every two epochs during training.

\subsubsection{SAM 3}

The second model was SAM 3 \citep{carion2026sam3}, which combines prompt-conditioned detection and segmentation with a shared Perception Encoder for visual and language representations. Only image-level inference was considered because orthomosaic tiles were processed independently.

Two SAM 3 regimes were evaluated. First, the pretrained model was applied in zero-shot mode using the text prompt ``shrub'', without domain-specific training. This experiment provided a baseline for assessing the transferability of the pretrained representation to overhead shrub imagery.

Second, SAM 3 was adapted to the JuniperMapper domain. The vision and language backbones were kept frozen, and only the detection and segmentation components were fine-tuned on the PI training subset, using "shrub" as the text prompt. Training ran for 30 epochs with a batch size of 2 and bfloat16 mixed precision. The trainable parameters were optimized with AdamW, using a base learning rate of \num{8e-5} with an inverse-square-root schedule, 20 warm-up steps and a final cooldown. Weight decay was 0.1, excluding biases and normalization layers, and gradients were clipped at a norm of 0.1. The objective function combined Hungarian-matched L1 and generalized-IoU bounding-box losses, a focal classification/presence loss, focal and Dice mask losses, and an auxiliary one-to-many matching term.

Data processing followed the original SAM 3 fine-tuning configuration. Training images were resized to a random size between 480 and 1008 pixels and padded to the model's 1008-pixel input resolution, and prompt boxes were jittered with Gaussian noise ($\sigma$ = 0.1, maximum displacement 20 pixels). Validation images were resized directly to 1008 pixels. The model was evaluated on the PI validation subset after each epoch.

\subsubsection{Computational environment}

Detectron2 Mask R-CNN and SAM 3 were trained and evaluated on a workstation equipped with an Intel Core i7 processor, 64 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 Ti GPU with 16 GB VRAM, under Ubuntu running through WSL2 on Windows 11 with CUDA acceleration.

\subsection{Post-training evaluation}

After model development, the selected checkpoint from each architecture was evaluated on the PI test and FW datasets. Following \citet{khaldi2024individual}, prediction performance was examined across a range of confidence-score thresholds to characterize the precision-recall trade-off and determine an appropriate operating point for deployment.

Performance was characterized using instance-level mask IoU and the directional S-IoU formulation proposed by \citet{khaldi2024individual}. For conventional IoU, predicted and reference instances were evaluated using their best-matching counterpart, and a detection was considered a true positive when the mask overlap exceeded the specified IoU threshold. IoU thresholds of 0.50 and 0.75 were considered.

S-IoU was additionally calculated to account for fragmentation and merging of shrub crowns. Rather than considering only the best-matching instance, S-IoU compares each prediction or reference object with the union of the overlapping instances in the opposite set. Prediction- and reference-directed evaluations were therefore performed separately to determine true positives, false positives, and false negatives.

Let $\mathcal{P} = \{P_1, \dots, P_m\}$ be the predicted masks retained at a given confidence threshold and $\mathcal{G} = \{G_1, \dots, G_n\}$ the reference masks of an image. A prediction and a reference mask were considered overlapping when they shared at least one pixel. For each prediction $P_i$, the prediction-directed S-IoU was computed against the union $G^{\cup}_i$ of all reference masks overlapping it:
\begin{equation}
    \text{S-IoU}_{P}(P_i) = \frac{\lvert P_i \cap G^{\cup}_i \rvert}{\lvert G^{\cup}_i \rvert},
    \qquad
    G^{\cup}_i = \bigcup_{j \,:\, \lvert P_i \cap G_j \rvert > 0} G_j .
    \label{eq:siou_pred}
\end{equation}
Similarly, for each reference mask $G_j$, the reference-directed S-IoU was computed against the union $P^{\cup}_j$ of all predictions overlapping it:
\begin{equation}
    \text{S-IoU}_{G}(G_j) = \frac{\lvert G_j \cap P^{\cup}_j \rvert}{\lvert G_j \rvert},
    \qquad
    P^{\cup}_j = \bigcup_{i \,:\, \lvert P_i \cap G_j \rvert > 0} P_i .
    \label{eq:siou_ref}
\end{equation}
S-IoU was set to 0 for objects without any overlapping counterpart. Given an evaluation threshold $\tau$, a prediction was counted as a true positive (TP) if $\text{S-IoU}_{P}(P_i) \geq \tau$ and as a false positive (FP) otherwise, and a reference mask was counted as a false negative (FN) if $\text{S-IoU}_{G}(G_j) < \tau$. Counts were accumulated over all images, and precision, recall and F1-score were calculated as
\begin{equation}
    \text{Precision} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}},
    \qquad
    \text{Recall} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}},
    \qquad
    \text{F1} = \frac{2 \cdot \text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}} .
    \label{eq:prf1}
\end{equation}
Because TPs and FNs are determined from different directions, TP + FN need not equal the number of reference masks.

Precision, recall, and F1-score were calculated across prediction-confidence thresholds for both the PI test and FW datasets. Consistent with \citet{khaldi2024individual}, the PI test dataset was used to characterize model performance within the photo-interpretation domain, whereas the independently field-derived FW dataset was used to characterize performance under conditions more representative of real-world deployment.

For each model, F1-score response curves across confidence thresholds were calculated separately for the PI test and FW datasets. The operational confidence threshold for subsequent deployment was selected using the FW dataset, as the field-based reference data provide the closest available approximation to the conditions encountered during large-area application. True positives were defined using an S-IoU threshold of 0.50. The operational point was defined as the highest confidence threshold for which the F1-score was within 0.01 of the maximum F1-score obtained on the FW dataset. This criterion favours more confident predictions and reduces false-positive detections while maintaining near-optimal F1 performance. The selected threshold and its corresponding precision, recall, and F1-score were reported together with the full confidence-threshold response curves.

\subsection{Independent study-area validation}

Field validation was conducted in the Encinacorba study area using 26 plots \qty{9}{\square\metre} distributed along two longitudinal transects. Within each plot, vegetation composition and percentage cover were recorded. Shrub and tree objects were subsequently delineated from the field-reference information and corresponding orthomosaic imagery, producing 58 shrub polygons and 21 tree polygons.

The reference shrubs included \textit{Lavandula stoechas}, \textit{Cistus laurifolius}, \textit{Erica vagans}, \textit{Rosa} sp., \textit{Jasonia glutinosa}, and \textit{Arctostaphylos uva-ursi}. Tree annotations included \textit{Quercus ilex}, \textit{Pinus pinaster}, and \textit{Crataegus monogyna}.

\subsection{Orthomosaic inference and mask reconstruction}

Each selected model was deployed over the orthomosaic using overlapping tiles of 448 pixels size with a 64-pixel overlap, matching the size of the JuniperMapper images used for training. The same tiling was used for both models. SAM 3 internally resizes every input to its fixed 1008-pixel resolution, so each tile was upsampled exactly as the 448-pixel validation images had been during domain adaptation, keeping shrubs at the scale used for checkpoint selection. Tiles were passed to the models as unmodified 8-bit RGB, and pixels outside the orthomosaic footprint were excluded using its alpha band. Predicted instance masks were converted to georeferenced polygons, and detections from overlapping tiles were merged into a single shrub map per model. Instances smaller than 4 pixels (\qty{0.01}{\square\metre}) were discarded.

Predicted masks were converted to georeferenced polygons. To reduce tile-boundary fragmentation, polygons from different inference windows that intersected, touched or were separated by no more than 0.05~m were grouped and unioned, and small gaps between the merged parts were closed. Polygons from the same window were not merged. The maximum, mean and median confidence scores of each group were retained.

The resulting shrub maps were compared with the field reference. Because most reference polygons extended beyond the plots, no geometry was clipped: each reference polygon was matched against all predictions, and a matched prediction was counted as a true positive wherever it lay. Since all shrubs within each plot were digitized, an unmatched prediction was counted as a false positive only when at least half of its area lay inside a plot; outside the plots, it could correspond to an undigitized shrub.

Predicted shrubs were matched to reference shrub polygons using greedy one-to-one matching with an IoU threshold of 0.10. Precision was calculated as the proportion of true positives among true and false positives, recall as the proportion of reference shrubs successfully matched, and F1 as the harmonic mean of precision and recall.

Tree commission was calculated as the proportion of reference tree polygons matched by a prediction, using the same one-to-one matching (IoU $\geq$ 0.10) applied separately to the tree polygons. It describes where part of the false positives fell and is not a separate test.

\subsection{SAM 3 prompt sensitivity analysis}

The text prompt ``shrub'' was defined as the primary SAM 3 prompt before any evaluation. It was the category name used to domain-adapt the SAM 3 decoder and to select the checkpoint on the validation split, and it was used for the main comparison between architectures.

A supplementary prompt-sensitivity analysis then evaluated nine alternative short noun phrases describing shrub vegetation: ``bush'', ``low shrub'', ``small shrub'', ``small bush'', ``woody shrub'', ``Mediterranean shrub'', ``dry shrub'', ``burned shrub'' and ``brown shrub''. To keep the analysis consistent with model development, each prompt was scored with the same data and metric used for checkpoint selection: the JuniperMapper Photo Interpretation validation split (67 images) and COCO mask AP50, with the selected checkpoint and all other settings unchanged.

\subsection{LiDAR processing and canopy-height modelling}

The UAV LiDAR point cloud was preprocessed in R Statistical Software (v4.6.1; \citealp{rsoftware}) using the lidR R package (v.4.3.2; \citealp{lidr2020}, \citealp{lidr2026}). Statistical Outlier Removal was first applied using 10 nearest neighbours and a three-standard-deviation rejection threshold.

Three ground-classification approaches were evaluated: Multiscale Curvature Classification (MCC), Progressive Morphological Filtering (PMF), and Progressive TIN Densification (PTD). Ground-classification performance was assessed qualitatively using elevation profiles along four representative transects and quantitatively against surveyed GNSS elevations.

For quantitative assessment, LiDAR-derived digital terrain models (DTMs) for each classification method of each GNSS reference point were extracted. Elevation residuals were calculated relative to the surveyed orthometric elevation, and root mean square error was computed for each target. Mean RMSE across the GNSS targets was used to select the ground-classification method subsequently used for point-cloud normalization.

The resulting CHM had a spatial resolution of 0.25~m and was aligned to the orthomosaic grid. This resolution was chosen because at 5 cm only \qty{5.5}{\percent} of the cells contained a direct LiDAR return, compared with \qty{69}{\percent} at 0.25~m. Cells without returns were retained as NoData rather than interpolated.

\subsection{LiDAR-based refinement of RGB predictions}

The CHM was used as a post-processing layer and was not supplied as an input to any deep-learning model, so the same refinement could be applied independently to the predictions of both architectures. Because CHM heights close to the ground were within the LiDAR noise, the CHM was used only to separate trees from shrubs; predictions dominated by bare ground were not filtered and were left to the model confidence threshold.

For each predicted polygon, the CHM cells whose centres fell inside the polygon were extracted. From them, the number of valid cells, the coverage (proportion of cells with a valid height), the mean, maximum and 90th-percentile height (P90), and the tall-cell fraction were calculated. The tall-cell fraction was the proportion of valid cells exceeding a height threshold h and the tall-cell fraction, i.e. the proportion of valid cells exceeding a height threshold h. The same h was used in the rejection rule below.

Each prediction was then assigned to one of four post-processing categories, evaluated in the following order:

- Insufficient support. Predictions with a coverage below 0.3 or fewer than five valid cells were retained without modification. Predictions smaller than one CHM cell or outside the CHM extent have no valid cells and fall into this category.
- Rejected. Predictions with P90 \> h and a tall-cell fraction $\geq$ f were removed as trees.  
- Clipped. Predictions with at least one cell above h that did not meet the rejection rule had their tall cells removed, and the largest remaining connected part was retained. If that part was smaller than \qty{0.25}{\square\metre}, the prediction was rejected.  
- Unchanged. All other predictions were retained as they were.  

The thresholds h and f were calibrated on the 79 field-reference vegetation masks: 58 shrubs and 21 trees, with the growth form assigned from the species identified in the field. All combinations of h $\in$ \{1.3, 1.5, 1.7, 1.9\}~m and f $\in$ \{0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5\} were applied to the masks with the procedure above. The selection rule was defined before calibration: among the combinations that neither rejected nor clipped any shrub mask, the one that rejected most tree masks was chosen, with ties resolved in favour of the higher h and then the higher f. For comparison, a less strict rule that allowed shrub masks to be clipped, but not rejected, was also evaluated.

Because the thresholds were selected on the same masks, the selection was repeated in a leave-one-ROI-out procedure. Each mask was assigned to the \qty{9}{\square\metre} field ROI it overlapped most, or to the nearest ROI if it overlapped none (three masks, at most 0.85~m away). For each of the 26 ROIs, the thresholds were selected on the masks of the remaining ROIs and applied to the held-out masks.

The selected parameters were then applied unchanged to the predictions of both architectures. Performance before and after LiDAR refinement was compared using the same field-reference validation procedure.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Model development and selection}

\subsubsection{Detectron2 Mask R-CNN}

The Detectron2 model was evaluated on the PI validation subset seven times during the 4,000 training iterations, plus once at the final iteration. Mask AP50 increased from 0.789 at iteration 569 to a maximum of 0.845 at iteration 1,709, and AP50:95 from 0.523 to 0.563. AP50 then decreased slightly and stabilised at 0.830-0.842 until the end of training, while AP50:95 remained between 0.560 and 0.565. The checkpoint at iteration 1,709 was therefore retained. The highest AP50:95 (0.565, iteration 2,279) differed from that of the selected checkpoint by only 0.002, so the secondary criterion was not needed.

\subsubsection{SAM 3 domain adaptation}

Domain adaptation of SAM 3 was monitored on the PI validation subset using mask AP50 and AP50:95. Validation performance improved rapidly during the first epochs and subsequently reached a plateau (Table XX). AP50 increased from 0.852 at epoch 1 to 0.885 at epoch 4, while AP50:95 increased from 0.613 to 0.638 over the same interval. From epochs 4 to 18, performance remained comparatively stable, with AP50 ranging from 0.877 to 0.884 and AP50:95 from 0.635 to 0.647.

The highest validation performance was obtained at epoch 19, with a mask AP50 of 0.8904 and an AP50:95 of 0.6480. This checkpoint exceeded the previous best AP50 obtained at epoch 4 (0.885) and also achieved the highest AP50:95 observed during training. Model selection was based primarily on maximum validation AP50, with AP50:95 used as a secondary criterion in the event of a tie. Consequently, epoch 19 was retained as the final domain-adapted SAM 3 checkpoint.

Although the training schedule was configured for 30 epochs, optimization became unstable during epoch 20. After 5,491 training steps, corresponding to approximately 76 batches into epoch 20, the matching cost became non-finite. Subsequent batches were skipped by the trainer, resulting in 3,059 skipped batches over the remainder of the scheduled run. Accordingly, the zero AP values recorded for epochs 20-30 do not represent a genuine deterioration in model performance, but rather the absence of effective parameter updates following numerical failure.

Epoch 19 therefore represents both the highest-performing validation checkpoint and the last checkpoint obtained before training instability. Its weights were retained and were used as the selected domain-adapted SAM 3 model. Checkpoints generated after the onset of instability were excluded from subsequent model comparison because they did not represent valid continuation of the training process.

\subsection{Held-out JuniperMapper evaluation}

After model selection, the fixed Detectron2 and domain-adapted SAM 3 models were evaluated on the FW and PI test datasets. The FW results are presented first because the deployment operating points were selected on them.

\subsubsection{Field-work dataset performance}

Response of F1 to the confidence threshold. On the FW dataset, the F1 response to the prediction-confidence threshold differed markedly between the two architectures (Fig. XX). At an evaluation threshold of \qty{50}{\percent}, Detectron2 Mask R-CNN rose steeply from F1 = \qty{15}{\percent} (IoU) and \qty{17}{\percent} (S-IoU) at a confidence of \qty{0}{\percent}, where all 31,744 candidate predictions were retained, to a broad plateau. F1 peaked at \qty{35}{\percent} confidence (IoU \qty{70.4}{\percent}; S-IoU \qty{75.8}{\percent}) and remained within 1 percentage point of this maximum between \qty{20}{\percent} and \qty{45}{\percent}. Above \qty{60}{\percent} confidence it declined gradually, and above \qty{85}{\percent} more steeply, reaching approximately \qty{46}{\percent} at \qty{95}{\percent}. The domain-adapted SAM 3 model showed a unimodal, sharply peaked response. F1 increased progressively from \qty{22}{\percent} at \qty{0}{\percent} confidence, where precision was low because of the large number of low-scoring predictions, to a maximum at \qtyrange{55}{60}{\percent} (IoU \qty{74.9}{\percent}; S-IoU \qty{78.3}{\percent}). It then fell rapidly to \qty{60}{\percent} at \qty{80}{\percent} confidence and \qty{5}{\percent} at \qty{95}{\percent}, because few predictions received scores above \qty{85}{\percent}. Sensitivity to the choice of operating point therefore differed between models: a departure of 10 percentage points from the optimum reduced F1 by up to 3.8 points for SAM 3 and by less than 1 point for Detectron2, whereas a departure of 20 points reduced F1 by up to 17.8 points for SAM 3 but by only about 2 points for Detectron2.

Stricter evaluation threshold. At an evaluation threshold of \qty{75}{\percent}, F1 decreased for both models, but the shape of each response was largely preserved. SAM 3 again achieved higher values (S-IoU \qty{68.4}{\percent} at \qty{60}{\percent} confidence; IoU \qty{53.6}{\percent} at \qty{65}{\percent}) than Detectron2 (S-IoU \qty{64.2}{\percent} at \qty{35}{\percent}; IoU \qty{45.3}{\percent} at \qty{80}{\percent}). For Detectron2, IoU-based F1 increased slightly with confidence. This indicates that higher-scoring predictions delineated crown boundaries more accurately.

IoU versus S-IoU. S-IoU-based F1 exceeded IoU-based F1 at every confidence threshold for both models. The difference was moderate at the \qty{50}{\percent} evaluation threshold (approximately 3-5 percentage points) but increased to 15-20 points at \qty{75}{\percent}. This suggests that a substantial share of errors under the stricter criterion arose from fragmented or merged crowns and boundary misplacement rather than from undetected shrubs. For Detectron2 and SAM 3, the IoU and S-IoU curves converged at high confidence thresholds. Fragmentation and merging errors were therefore concentrated among lower-confidence predictions, whereas the most confident predictions generally corresponded to single, well-delineated crowns.

Operating-point selection. Applying the selection criterion (the highest confidence threshold whose S-IoU@\qty{50}{\percent} F1 was within 1 percentage point of the maximum) gave operating points of \qty{60}{\percent} for SAM 3 and \qty{45}{\percent} for Detectron2 (Table XX).

\subsection{Calibration of the LiDAR refinement rule}

CHM measurements were available for all 79 field-reference masks: 58 shrubs (46 Lavandula stoechas, 8 Cistus laurifolius, 4 Erica vagans) and 21 trees (13 Quercus ilex, 6 Pinus pinaster, 2 Crataegus monogyna). The median number of valid CHM cells per mask was 15 for shrubs and 96 for trees. With the minimum-support criteria, 10 shrub masks, all of them small L. stoechas, had insufficient LiDAR support and would be retained without modification. No tree mask fell into this category.

Mean CHM height differed clearly between growth forms (Figure 8). Q. ilex (2.87 m) and P. pinaster (2.45 m) were the tallest species. They were followed by C. monogyna (1.00 m), whose mean height was closer to that of the tallest shrub, C. laurifolius (0.67 m), than to the other tree species. E. vagans (0.21 m) and L. stoechas (0.13 m) were the lowest.

The species means hid an overlap at mask level. Six tree masks had mean heights below 1 m: three Q. ilex, two P. pinaster and one C. monogyna. That range was also reached by C. laurifolius masks (up to 1.03 m). The 90th-percentile height (P90) ranged from 0 to 1.46 m in shrub masks and from 1.35 to 6.75 m in tree masks. No CHM cell inside any shrub mask exceeded 1.63 m. The CHM also contained negative values down to −0.05 m, a noise level close to the mean height of L. stoechas (0.13 m).

\subsection{Exploratory SAM 3 prompt analysis}

On the Photo Interpretation validation split, the text prompt changed SAM 3's accuracy only slightly unless it added a colour or condition modifier (Table X). The predefined prompt "shrub" reached a mask AP50 of 0.890 and a mask mAP50:95 of 0.648, the same values recorded when the checkpoint was selected. Six of the alternative prompts ("low shrub", "bush", "Mediterranean shrub", "small bush", "woody shrub" and "small shrub") scored within 0.006 mask AP50 of "shrub" (0.885-0.889). Their mask mAP50:95 was equally close (0.643-0.651). No single prompt ranked first on both metrics: "shrub" had the highest mask AP50 and "woody shrub" the highest mAP50:95, and both leads are smaller than 0.004. On a 67-image validation set, such differences do not support preferring any synonym over the predefined prompt.

Prompts that added a colour or condition modifier performed consistently worse. Compared with "shrub", mask AP50 fell by 0.018 for "dry shrub", by 0.027 for "burned shrub" and by 0.102 for "brown shrub". "Brown shrub" also had the lowest mask mAP50:95 (0.561, against 0.648 for "shrub").

Because the alternative prompts were evaluated on the same validation split used to select the SAM 3 checkpoint with the "shrub" prompt, the analysis was treated as exploratory rather than as a post-hoc prompt optimisation. The predefined prompt "shrub" was therefore retained for the principal inter-model comparison.

Visual inspection identified two recurrent error types. \section{Discussion}

DETECTRON2' MODEL INTERNAL EVALUATION. As in Khaldi et al., the two metrics follow a similar shape, but S-IoU's F1-score is consistently higher than IoU's — for example, on the PI test set at θ\_score \= \qty{80}{\percent}, F1 reaches \qty{84.8}{\percent} under IoU@50 versus \qty{88.6}{\percent} under S-IoU@50 — because S-IoU credits a prediction against the union of all overlapping ground-truth instances rather than requiring a single one-to-one match, so fragmented or merged canopy predictions still count as (partial) successes. This directional definition also means that, for S-IoU, the true positives entering the precision calculation are not the same instances as those entering the recall calculation: precision uses prediction-directed matches (each prediction scored against its unioned overlapping ground truths), while recall and its false negatives are computed independently, label-directed (each ground truth scored against its unioned overlapping predictions). As a result, TP \+ FN does not equal the total number of ground-truth instances for the S-IoU rows below, unlike the IoU rows, where one-to-one matching guarantees the two are consistent.

SAM 3 Zero-Shot BAD FOR DOMAIN SPECIFIC TASKS. Zero-shot AP falls from 56.4 (COCO) to 15.2 on a 100-domain specialized benchmark (Dabaja and Çelík 2026), i.e. zero-shot text-prompt segmentation degrades sharply outside SAM 3's pretraining distribution and cannot be assumed to transfer to vegetation/remote-sensing imagery without domain-specific evaluation. In an orchard fruit-annotation study (Wang, 2026), SAM 3 reached higher recall than SAM (0.91 vs. 0.81) but markedly lower precision (0.29 vs. 0.42) — i.e. SAM 3's dominant failure mode is over-segmentation / false positives, not missed detections. In remote sensing, text prompts inject a ground-level semantic bias that degrades coordinate regression on overhead imagery, while visual (exemplar) prompts align well to overhead geometry (Dabaja and Çelík 2026). Our imagery (Zenmuse L1 orthomosaics, 5 cm GSD) is exactly this overhead case.

SAM 3 has evaluated internally with shrub prompt, but then several one has been tested. There could be a slightly decrease in performance because the confidence value to select masks has not been set programmatically with each prompt. Maybe account that in further research.

Notably, the best domain-adapted SAM 3 checkpoints modestly outperform the Detectron2 reproduction (Section 4.1) at matched IoU@50/S-IoU@50 operating points on both test sets: \qty{88.5}{\percent} vs. \qty{84.8}{\percent} F1 (IoU@50) on PI, and \qty{74.9}{\percent} vs. \qty{70.4}{\percent} on FW. This suggests that, despite starting from a much weaker zero-shot baseline, lightweight frozen-backbone decoder fine-tuning lets SAM 3 match or exceed a fully fine-tuned Mask R-CNN on this dataset — provided the checkpoint is taken before the numerical instability identified above (epoch 19 was the last checkpoint before it).

The Detectron2 Mask R-CNN model trained in this study closely matched the performance reported for the original JuniperMapper model (Khaldi et al., 2024), which used the same ResNet101-C4 backbone and the same PI and FW test sets. Comparing the best F1 achieved on each dataset (Table A1), our model was within 1.7 percentage points of the original on the PI test set across all four evaluation criteria (e.g. IoU@\qty{50}{\percent} \qty{84.8}{\percent} vs \qty{84.84}{\percent}; S-IoU@\qty{50}{\percent} \qty{88.6}{\percent} vs \qty{87.87}{\percent}). On the FW dataset it was 1.1–2.6 points lower at the \qty{50}{\percent} evaluation threshold and at IoU@\qty{75}{\percent}, and 4.1 points lower for S-IoU@\qty{75}{\percent} (\qty{64.2}{\percent} vs \qty{68.32}{\percent}). At the original study's confidence thresholds (\qty{90}{\percent} for PI and \qty{50}{\percent} for FW), our model was 1.5–4.4 points lower. It retained fewer predictions (543 vs 646 on PI and 1,345 vs 1,706 on FW), with higher precision and lower recall. Our model therefore assigned lower confidence scores than the original, which shifted its FW optimum from \qty{50}{\percent} to \qty{35}{\percent}, while its achievable segmentation performance remained comparable. This difference in score behaviour between two training runs of the same architecture supports deriving operating points separately for each model rather than adopting a fixed value from previous work. The confidence-threshold responses were also qualitatively consistent with the original study. On the PI data, F1 increased with confidence and peaked at a high threshold (\qty{80}{\percent} vs \qty{90}{\percent}). On the FW data, F1 rose rapidly and then formed a broad plateau, although ours extended over a narrower range (\qtyrange{20}{45}{\percent} vs \qtyrange{20}{85}{\percent}). Both studies showed a similar decrease from PI to FW data at the \qty{50}{\percent} evaluation threshold (14.4 and 12.8 points for IoU and S-IoU, vs 12.45 and 11.01). This indicates that the domain shift between photo-interpreted and field-based references is reproducible. At the \qty{75}{\percent} evaluation threshold, however, the S-IoU decrease was larger in our study (20.8 vs 16.5 points), suggesting that our model fragmented or merged field-observed crowns more often. The domain-adapted SAM 3 model exceeded the original JuniperMapper performance under seven of the eight criteria, by 1.4–6.6 points, and matched it for FW S-IoU@\qty{75}{\percent} (\qty{68.4}{\percent} vs \qty{68.32}{\percent}). The largest gains were for IoU@\qty{75}{\percent} (PI \qty{80.5}{\percent} vs \qty{73.95}{\percent}; FW \qty{53.6}{\percent} vs \qty{47.91}{\percent}), indicating more accurate crown delineation rather than only more detections.

All ground-classification algorithms placed the terrain surface above the GNSS reference: 25 of the 26 targets had a positive error for every method. This consistent positive bias suggests that low vegetation, such as herbaceous cover, small shrubs and litter, was partly kept in the ground class. Dense low vegetation can stop the laser before it reaches the soil, so the lowest available return marks the top of that layer rather than the ground surface. The more conservative filters reduced this effect. PMF had the smallest bias (0.356 m) and MCC and PTD 40°/3 m the largest (0.500 m and 0.517 m), which matches their different tolerance for low objects close to the terrain. The largest single error (about 2.6 m for every method) occurred at target 31, located at the centre of a Quercus ilex crown. There the dense evergreen canopy blocked the laser from reaching the soil, so none of the algorithms could find reliable ground points beneath it. The interpolated DTM was then controlled by returns from the crown or its edges. Excluding this target lowers the RMSE to 0.346 m for PMF, 0.416 m for PTD 30°/2 m, 0.530 m for MCC and 0.618 m for PTD 40°/3 m, and the mean error to 0.267–0.436 m. The ranking of the methods stays the same. Part of the remaining bias may also come from a systematic vertical offset between the GNSS and LiDAR height references, which this design cannot separate from the vegetation effect.

Picos et al. (2020), for example, used 0.25 m for the ground–shrub division and 2 and 3 m for shrub–stem separation in two UAV-LiDAR \*Eucalyptus\* plantation plots. However, this work only serves as a justification of LiDAR refinement operation. The thresholds come from a different system, and is not a safe start here. The median valid cells per mask is 15 for shrubs and 96 for trees. That is still small for tiny *Lavandula stoechas* masks, but usable. Three trees are only \`keep\`. Ids 65 (\*Quercus ilex\*, \`h\_p90\` of 1.35), 60 (\*Pinus pinaster\*, 1.44) and 44 (\*Crataegus monogyna\*, 1.37) have a \`tall\_fraction\` of 0\. Their heights overlap \*Cistus laurifolius\* (up to 1.46), so height alone cannot remove them. Trees 44 and 65 were also the trees still matched by predictions after the refinement of the deployed maps. These could be low resprouts after the 2003 fire. The models mostly predict taller vegetation and miss the low \*Lavandula\*. That is a recall and domain problem, and CHM filtering cannot fix it.

Precision rests on 1 to 8 scored predictions, so the values aren't reliable enough to rank the models. The Detectron2 precision of 1.000 after refinement rests on a single prediction that mainly covers a *Crataegus monogyna* tree and matches a *Lavandula stoechas* polygon with an IoU of only 0.107. Because shrub masks are not well predicted across all plots, the LiDAR refinement worked to remove the tree commission but not to improve the masks of the few well detected shrubs. The LiDAR is not proposed for the proposal of new shrub locations because the thresholds are validated with the annotations, so there is no way to assess the gains of this process without a bias.

Each model is deployed with its own checkpoint and its own confidence threshold, 0.45 for Detectron2 and 0.60 for SAM 3, and not with a single common value. The scores of the two architectures are not calibrated on the same scale, so a shared threshold would not make the comparison fairer. The consequence is that the deployment results reflect the operating point of each model as well as the architecture itself: a lower threshold keeps more low-confidence masks, which increases the number of predictions and the chance of a match at the cost of precision, and a higher one does the opposite.

\section{Conclusions}

1\. The refinement is a tree-commission fix. It removes most masks that land on trees (tree commission down \qtyrange{77}{83}{\percent}) without losing any matched shrub, so it is safe to apply. Precision rises only because false positives are removed from a very small set of predictions, and F1 stays below 0.1 because recall does not change.  
2\. More CHM filtering won't help. Tightening the height rule would only start removing true shrubs (\*Cistus\* and \*Crataegus\* reach 1.5 m), and the FPs mostly sit in the 0.3–1.7 m band.  
3\. The bottleneck is the models. Recall of \qtyrange{1.7}{3.4}{\percent} means they hit 1 to 2 of the 58 annotated shrubs. \*\*Check whether these are matching problems (IoU 0.1 is already lenient) or species/height mismatches\*\*.  
4\. \*\*Look and describe the remaining FPs\*\*. Overlay the retained SAM 3 masks on the orthomosaic to see what they are. If many are real shrubs that were never digitized, the ground truth is the limit, not the model.  Detectron2's only hit is a matching artefact, and SAM 3's two hits are real C. laurifolius crowns.
